Our purpose is to understand the conditions and decisions that allowed the incident, agree on changes that reduce both the chance and the impact of recurrence, and leave with named owners and verification dates. This is not a performance review of the individuals who responded. People acted with the information, tools, and incentives available at the time. If our conclusion is simply that someone should have noticed sooner or typed a different command, we will have missed the organizational and technical lessons. I’ll frame the impact and decisions, our Technical Lead will walk through the system behavior and remediation, and our Customer Support Lead will cover what customers experienced and how our communication performed.

The incident began at 9:42 a.m. Pacific last Thursday and customer impact ended at 11:18 a.m., a total of ninety-six minutes. During that period, approximately thirty-eight percent of interactive login attempts failed. Existing sessions remained valid, and API traffic using long-lived service credentials was mostly unaffected. The highest impact was on customers whose identity providers require signed authentication requests. Twenty-three enterprise accounts opened support cases, and three customers activated their own severity-one process. We have found no evidence of unauthorized access, data exposure, or lost writes. This was an availability incident, not a confidentiality or integrity incident, but the severity of the login failure and the loss of customer trust justify our highest operational classification.

Before we debate solutions, I want to confirm the shared timeline. At 9:15 the identity team began a planned certificate rotation. At 9:37 the authentication fleet started refreshing its signing configuration. At 9:42 metadata advertising the new public certificate became visible to customers while part of the fleet still used the old private key. Our first alert fired four minutes later. The on-call engineer acknowledged it at 9:48, support connected the first enterprise reports at 9:54, and an incident commander joined at 10:01. The certificate mismatch was identified at 10:17. The first rollback attempt did not resolve the issue because external identity providers had cached the new metadata. The team then converged the fleet on the new key through controlled restarts, with recovery beginning at 10:52 and the final stale instance leaving service at 11:07.

I want to explicitly recognize what went well. Detection and acknowledgment were inside our objectives. Support correlated separate customer reports quickly instead of treating them as unrelated account problems. Security joined early, helped rule out forged requests, and preserved the evidence needed for this review. Once the mismatch was understood, the response team resisted restarting the entire fleet at once and used batches to protect existing sessions. The incident commander maintained a decision log and communications continued on a regular cadence even when diagnosis was incomplete. Those behaviors limited the blast radius and gave us a reliable record. We should preserve them while fixing the structural weaknesses that made diagnosis and recovery slower than necessary.

The central lesson is that our rotation process encoded a timing assumption where it needed a compatibility protocol and an observable state transition. The runbook assumed secret refresh would complete within five minutes because it usually had before. It did not define a measurable completion condition, and no single owner was accountable for the full transition across the signer configuration and public metadata. The deployment check proved that new instances could sign with the new key; it did not prove that every serving instance had stopped using the old one. The rollback model was inherited from application deployments, where returning to an earlier version normally restores compatibility. That mental model does not hold when external systems cache public metadata.

There are three high-priority action groups. First, we will redesign rotation around an overlap window. The new public certificate will be published alongside the current certificate before any signer uses the new private key. Representative external providers must fetch that dual-certificate metadata before the fleet changes. After telemetry proves every active signer uses the new fingerprint, the old certificate will remain available through a defined cache-expiration period. Only then can automation remove it. Our Technical Lead owns the design and implementation, with a target before the next production rotation in six weeks.

Second, we will add evidence and guardrails to the deployment path. The pipeline must expose desired and observed key versions for every active instance. It must block retirement of a certificate while any signatures still use that fingerprint. Synthetic authentication will run against several external identity providers with different cache behavior, not only our internal provider. Dashboards and alerts will group failures by provider, region, application version, and signing fingerprint. Completion means an on-call engineer can identify a mixed fleet within five minutes during a staging exercise; it does not merely mean that a dashboard or alert definition has been merged.

Third, we will improve the operating model around the change. The signer deployment and metadata publication steps will become one versioned workflow with an end-to-end owner, reviewer, verification window, and cache-aware recovery plan. The escalation alias will be generated from the current on-call schedule instead of being maintained by hand. Before the next rotation, we will run a game day that deliberately leaves part of the fleet stale, simulates a provider with a long metadata cache, and practices recovery. Engineering, SRE, security, support, and communications will all participate, because the incident exposed coordination gaps as well as a software flaw.

We should also look beyond certificates. This failure belongs to a broader class of distributed transitions where producers and consumers cannot update atomically. Feature flags, encryption keys, webhook secrets, schema versions, and network policy changes may contain similar undocumented propagation assumptions. Architecture will lead a two-week review focused on transitions that can enter an incompatible intermediate state and cause broad customer impact. The goal is not a giant speculative backlog. We will prioritize cases where overlap, version negotiation, or observable convergence can materially reduce risk, and each accepted item will have a concrete test showing the transition is safe under delay.

On customer follow-up, our Customer Support Lead will coordinate with account teams for the affected enterprises. The three customers who declared severity one need a direct summary of impact, confirmation that we found no security breach, the corrective-action plan, and a clear path for service-credit or security-documentation questions. Product and support will review whether any administrators need help validating their identity-provider configuration. We will publish the internal review after factual corrections today, then prepare an external version that is candid about the failure mode without exposing sensitive operational details. The external document must distinguish completed mitigation from planned prevention.

For accountability, every action will have one owner, a date, and a verification artifact. “Improve monitoring” is not complete when a chart exists; it is complete when the game day triggers the alert, the responder reaches the fingerprint breakdown directly, and the mixed fleet is identified within the target time. “Update the runbook” is not complete when prose is merged; it is complete when a team unfamiliar with the implementation can follow it without ambiguous decisions. We will track these actions in the incident record, review high-priority work in two weeks, and keep the incident open until the overlap workflow and staging exercise pass.

My final assessment is that this outage was preventable, but not because one person ignored an obvious instruction. Our system validated the destination and not the journey. It depended on an unmeasured refresh delay, exposed only one verification certificate, and used a rollback strategy that ignored external caches. The response team mitigated responsibly once the failure mode became visible. I am asking this group to approve the three action groups, their owners, and the six-week deadline.
