I am {{name}}, the Privacy and Security Lead reviewing Decision Digest for launch. My role today is to determine whether the proposed experience gives organizations and meeting participants meaningful control over how their conversation is processed, whether our technical controls match the claims in our documentation, and whether the rollout can be stopped before a small issue becomes a broad trust event. I am not evaluating whether summaries are commercially attractive; the pilot already shows that. I am evaluating whether the feature can deliver that value within clear purpose, access, retention, transparency, and incident-response boundaries. My current position is conditional approval for the five-customer cohort, subject to four evidence items I will describe.

The allowed purpose is narrow: produce an editable, transcript-linked draft of decisions, action items, and open questions for the completed meeting. Meeting content cannot be used for advertising, employee scoring, generalized model training, or unrelated workspace profiling. The generation provider operates as a processor under our agreement and may not retain prompts or outputs beyond the configured transient window. We send only the transcript segments needed for the current digest, plus speaker labels already visible in the meeting. We do not send participant email addresses, workspace billing data, private chat, or content from other meetings. Any future purpose, especially coaching or cross-meeting analytics, requires a separate review and cannot be introduced through a prompt change.

Consent and notice require multiple layers because administrator enablement alone is insufficient for a live conversation. An organization administrator first enables the feature and accepts the data-processing terms. The organizer then chooses Decision Digest for a specific meeting. Every attendee receives a persistent in-meeting notice before processing eligibility is established, and the meeting record shows whether that notice was delivered. If delivery fails, generation must not begin. The organizer cannot dismiss the notice on behalf of others, and a hidden background setting cannot silently reactivate the feature. For external or anonymous attendees, which are outside launch scope, admission rejects the job rather than assuming the organizer’s policy extends to them.

The most important unresolved workflow is a participant who objects after the meeting. Our policy allows that person to request deletion through the organization administrator or our privacy channel. For the launch scope, we will delete the entire derived digest rather than attempt to remove one speaker’s contribution while preserving an output influenced by it. The source recording follows the organization’s separate meeting-retention policy, and our interface must not imply that deleting the digest deletes the source recording. Customer Success has drafted the request procedure, Engineering has implemented deletion propagation for derived data, and Legal has reviewed the wording. We still need one end-to-end exercise proving that the request reaches the correct workspace, triggers the right deletion, and produces a completion record.

Retention must be understandable. Temporary transcript windows and provider-side request data expire after processing and may remain in encrypted operational backup for no more than the documented recovery period. The digest remains with the meeting until the customer deletes it or the meeting itself expires. Derived embeddings used only to assemble the digest follow the shorter processing retention, not the meeting’s longer retention. Support logs must use identifiers and error categories rather than transcript excerpts. Debug access to content requires an approved customer case, time-limited authorization, and an audit record. The launch runbook will prohibit copying meeting text into general incident channels, ticket titles, or unapproved analysis tools.

Access control inherits the meeting record’s audience. A user who cannot open the original meeting cannot open or export its digest. When meeting membership changes, digest access changes with it. Organizers may edit the draft, but edits do not grant a wider audience. Shared links use the same organization controls as recordings and can be revoked. The security test covered direct-object references, role changes, expired sessions, cross-organization identifiers, and export authorization. No high-severity finding remains open. One medium finding involving stale link previews was fixed and retested. We will repeat the authorization suite against the release candidate because access behavior is too important to infer from unit coverage alone.

Export auditing is a launch gate because exporting changes the risk boundary. Reading a digest inside the controlled meeting experience is not equivalent to downloading a portable file. Administrators need to know who exported which digest and when, especially during an investigation or deletion request. The event should contain actor, organization, meeting and digest identifiers, timestamp, result, and format, but no meeting content. Engineering’s proposed event satisfies that design. My team will verify success, denied export, and retried export in the customer-visible audit console. Operational telemetry that only our engineers can query does not satisfy the administrator promise.

Model quality is also a trust issue, even when it is not strictly a privacy control. A false decision attributed to a named person can affect reputation or workplace action. The launch design mitigates this by calling the output a draft, linking every item to transcript evidence, exposing uncertainty, allowing correction, and preventing automatic task creation. We should avoid anthropomorphic copy that suggests the assistant understood intent. Quality monitoring must look for unsupported claims and incorrect attribution by language, not merely user engagement. If a customer reports a harmful false attribution, support should preserve the minimal evidence, route it through the trust escalation, and avoid broad internal sharing of the meeting.

Our threat model includes malicious transcript content attempting to redirect the model, a participant speaking secrets aloud, an attacker guessing meeting identifiers, a compromised support account, provider unavailability, and an internal operator using debug tools outside an approved case. Prompt-injection tests confirm that transcript instructions cannot alter the output schema or request external tools. Output is treated as untrusted text and safely rendered. Meeting identifiers are authorization checked, not treated as bearer secrets. Support access is least privilege and time limited. Provider failure results in a failed or delayed digest, not rerouting to an unapproved model. Operator queries are audited and sampled by Security Operations.

Regional processing claims must match actual routing. The job carries the organization’s region from admission through transcript retrieval, model invocation, derived storage, and deletion. A region mismatch causes rejection and a security alert; it cannot silently fall back to another pool. Pilot routing tests passed for North America and Europe, including retry and dead-letter handling. The first cohort includes customers in both regions so we can verify real operational evidence. Sales must not promise additional residency locations until the full path exists. The capability matrix is the source of truth, and exceptions require Privacy, Security, Legal, and Engineering approval rather than an account-level configuration workaround.

For monitoring, I require a daily launch report containing notice-delivery failures, jobs rejected for policy mismatch, privileged content-access events, deletion completion, export events, provider retention attestations, and trust-related support cases. A single confirmed job processed without valid notice is a stop condition. A deletion that exceeds the contractual window, an unexplained regional mismatch, unauthorized access, or evidence of provider retention beyond policy also stops expansion and begins incident triage. Lower-severity quality issues may pause a language or organization while investigation continues. These thresholds must be encoded in the launch plan so the team does not debate acceptable risk while an event is unfolding.

My four approval conditions are concrete. First, the participant-withdrawal exercise must prove intake, identity verification, derived-data deletion, reconciliation, and completion notice. Second, the export event must appear correctly in the customer-visible audit log. Third, the release candidate must pass the authorization and regional-routing suites with no unresolved high-severity finding. Fourth, Product, Customer Success, and Sales must publish one consistent description of supported purpose, languages, retention, reviewability, and limitations. Evidence for each condition will be attached to the launch record, and my team will sign only after reviewing the artifact rather than accepting a status summary.

With those conditions, I support a bounded five-customer launch because the system is designed to fail closed on notice and policy errors, the rollout is reversible, and the feature avoids autonomous downstream action. I do not support unrestricted enablement on the calendar date regardless of evidence. Trust is not a disclaimer added after the product works; it is part of the product behavior customers are buying. If we maintain the narrow purpose, visible draft status, transcript grounding, access inheritance, deletion guarantees, and stop conditions, Decision Digest can reduce meeting work without turning participant conversation into an uncontrolled data source. I will own the privacy and security go or no-go decision and remain on the launch review through the first expansion.
