I am {{name}}, the Customer Success Lead for the Decision Digest launch. I’ll cover what design partners actually experienced, how we will select and enable the first production cohort, what support and account teams need to answer confidently, and which customer signals should pause expansion. I am aligned with the Product Manager’s recommendation for a five-customer conditional launch. My confidence depends less on the calendar date than on whether administrators understand the feature, attendees see the notice, users know the digest is a reviewable draft, and our teams can resolve a question without passing the customer through Product, Engineering, Privacy, and Support separately.

Across twenty-four design partners, the strongest response came from project teams with recurring planning meetings and distributed membership. Those teams used digests to confirm decisions, catch action items, and help absent colleagues find the relevant transcript moment. Users who opened at least three digests reported saving about fourteen minutes per meeting, and several stopped maintaining a parallel manual decision log. The weakest response came from informal brainstorming sessions, meetings with many external guests, and teams expecting a polished executive summary without review. That pattern supports the proposed scope. We should launch where the product’s structure matches the work rather than encouraging every meeting to generate more content by default.

Pilot feedback also shows why language matters. Customers trust “draft digest with transcript links” more than “AI meeting minutes.” The first phrase tells them what the feature does and how to verify it. The second sounds official and complete. Administrators asked whether summaries train a shared model, where content is processed, how long derived data remains, who can export it, and what happens when a participant objects. Organizers asked whether external guests are supported and whether action items sync automatically. End users asked how to correct names and whether edits change the transcript. Our enablement package must answer these questions directly, using the same capability matrix reviewed by Product and Privacy.

I propose five criteria for the first cohort. Each organization must already store meeting transcripts under a documented retention policy, have an administrator willing to complete the Decision Digest checklist, identify an internal owner for participant questions, use one of the supported regions, and nominate teams whose meetings fit the internal scheduled-meeting scope. We should include a mix of North American and European customers, English and at least two supported non-English languages, and both medium and large workspaces. We should exclude customers currently negotiating special retention terms or planning immediate external-meeting use. A launch cohort is an operating partnership, not simply the first five accounts whose sales representatives ask.

The administrator checklist will explain enablement, organizer choice, attendee notice, supported meeting types, access inheritance, export auditing, retention, deletion requests, and rollback behavior. The administrator must confirm where employees can find their organization’s recording policy and whom they should contact with a concern. We will provide a short organizer guide showing how to enable the feature, review the resulting draft, follow transcript links, correct attribution, and share intentionally. Attendee-facing help will explain why the notice appears and how to raise a question. None of this should require reading a legal memo. The test is whether a new administrator can accurately explain the lifecycle after a twenty-minute setup call.

Support readiness is organized around observable symptoms rather than internal components. The top cases we expect are a missing digest, delayed processing, an unsupported meeting, incorrect action attribution, a request to delete, a question about notice, an export-access problem, and uncertainty about regional processing. Each playbook begins with customer-safe facts to collect: organization, meeting identifier, approximate completion time, language, visible status, notice result, and error text. Agents should not request the full transcript in a ticket. Cases involving potential unauthorized access, missing notice, regional mismatch, harmful attribution, or overdue deletion enter dedicated escalations with fifteen-minute acknowledgment by the launch team.

We have trained the specialist support group and will train frontline agents before cohort invitations go out. Training uses eight simulated cases, including contradictory reports from an organizer and participant, a digest delayed by queue pressure, a German meeting with a missed action, and a participant who asks for derived content to be removed. Agents must locate the capability matrix, explain the distinction between digest and source-recording deletion, and route a trust escalation without copying meeting content. Customer Success managers will receive a shorter briefing focused on expectations and stakeholder communication. Sales will receive approved claims and a clear list of capabilities that are not yet available.

The consent-withdrawal workflow needs one accountable front door. A participant may contact the organization or us, but once the request reaches our team, the receiving agent owns it until the correct workspace administrator and privacy process are engaged. We verify identity using existing account procedures, identify the meeting without asking for unnecessary content, explain that the derived digest will be deleted while source-recording policy is separate, and give a completion expectation. Privacy decides whether additional action is required; Engineering supplies deletion evidence; Customer Success communicates status. The request must not bounce between queues simply because several teams contribute to resolution. Monday’s exercise will test handoffs and customer-facing language, not only backend deletion.

During the cohort, each organization will have a named Customer Success owner and backup. We will hold a thirty-minute enablement session, a check-in after the first ten digests, and a weekly review during the initial month. A shared launch channel will contain organization identifiers and operational status but no meeting text. The owner will summarize adoption, quality corrections, support cases, administrator questions, and any change in intended use. Product and Engineering can request a customer interview through that owner rather than contacting end users independently. This preserves context and avoids making pilot participants repeat the same concern to four internal teams.

Customer health metrics will complement the system dashboard. We will track eligible organizations enabled, eligible meetings using the feature, digest open rate, organizer edits, transcript-link use, shares, explicit feedback, and disablement. Support metrics include contacts per hundred enabled workspaces, first-response time, escalation volume, repeated contacts, and unresolved cases. Trust metrics include missing-notice reports, deletion requests, harmful attribution reports, and questions caused by inconsistent documentation. A high edit rate is not automatically failure; editing is part of the intended workflow. We need qualitative sampling to distinguish healthy refinement from a digest so inaccurate that users rewrite it.

Expansion should pause when customers cannot make safe sense of the experience, even if infrastructure remains green. My stop conditions are one confirmed missing attendee notice, any customer unable to disable generation as documented, a deletion request that misses its commitment, more than one substantiated unsupported decision per thousand digests, more than three support contacts per hundred enabled workspaces, or materially inconsistent answers from account and support teams. A cluster of confusion can justify a hold before it crosses a numerical line. I can trigger that hold, as can Engineering or Privacy, and the launch commander records the decision and customer communication.

The first-day communication plan is deliberately modest. Cohort administrators receive a direct invitation only after all launch gates are signed. The message states that this is staged general availability, describes supported scope, links the administrator checklist, and names their Customer Success contact. We will not announce unrestricted availability until the cohort review passes. If rollout pauses, administrators receive the known impact, what remains available, any action they should take, and the next update time. We should never call a pause “routine maintenance” if it is actually a quality, notice, or privacy investigation. Precise communication preserves credibility even when the product needs correction.

I own the administrator checklist, frontline training, cohort selection, escalation roster, and daily voice-of-customer report. Support Operations owns routing rules and case dashboards. Enablement owns the organizer and attendee guides. Each account owner must confirm the customer’s internal contact and intended meeting types before activation. By the go or no-go review, I will provide training completion, simulated-case results, signed cohort checklists, and the published escalation schedule. During the first week, I will join the daily launch review and bring both metrics and verbatim themes, with any customer excerpts minimized and approved for internal use.

My final recommendation is conditional go because customer value is established and the rollout has a narrow, supportable boundary. The remaining work is not cosmetic: export visibility, withdrawal handling, latency margin, and consistent claims are necessary for customers to trust the feature. If those artifacts pass, we can enroll five organizations and learn responsibly. If any gate fails, I will tell the cohort that we are moving the start date to complete a control, rather than asking them to absorb avoidable risk. Success is not merely that digests generate; it is that administrators and participants understand what happened, can verify the output, retain control, and know exactly where to go when something is wrong.
